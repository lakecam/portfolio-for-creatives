Poba

One good game night becomes a regular table

An iOS app that sets up small, recurring board game nights, so people who enjoyed playing together can keep meeting.

Role

Research, UX and UI

Context

Mentored Coursework

Timeline

Sep to Dec 2025

Status

Design Concept

Platform

Mobile iOS App

Problem

Young adults often struggle to make lasting friends

Most meetup and friendship apps are built around one-off events and interests listed on a profile, so strangers meet once, make small talk, and rarely see each other again.

Audience

Young adults looking for social connections

They want friends they see more than once, and would rather meet over a board game with a few others than through small talk on an app.

Research

Today’s options end after the first meetup

Bumble BFF chats rarely turn into plans, newcomers often go to a Meetup or Discord event once and never return, and BoardGameGeek has no local table. Meetup groups do recur, but they’re large and already settled, and Discord can at least point to a physical venue.

Competitors

Desk research

Game nights are scattered

Vancouver's game nights are split across several separate Meetup groups.

Stated tastes misplace newcomers

In a 2025 review, GameFor, a game-night app, grouped players by the games they said they liked, which newer players often misjudge, risking stressful nights.

First version

Match by personality

Zari, the 2025 version, used Myers-Briggs (MBTI) to avoid a night at the wrong table. It answered who to meet, but not what happens after.

Pivot

Interviews ruled out personality matching

All 4 interviewees saw personality matching as a gimmick, and a 2025 review flagged its intrusive questions and data-security risks. So the focus moved from matching people to helping a group keep meeting.

Finding

Personality matching read as a gimmick to interviewees: “I don’t want a gimmick, I just want to meet new people.”

Interviewee

Change

Tables form around play style, and “Run it back?” keeps the ones that click.

With only four interviews behind it, this direction still needs testing.

The loop

The night ends with a question

After each night, players answer “Run it back?”. Everyone who says yes joins a group, and the group votes on a date for the next night.

Decision 01

Tables grouped by play style

Dominant players can crowd out quieter ones, and that comes down to how each person plays, so sign-up asks about play style. Hosted nights with real players will show if this grouping works.

Decision 02

One answer for the whole table

Instead of rating each player, everyone privately answers one “Run it back?” question about the whole table, so quieter players aren’t ranked lower. The downside is that players can’t choose specific people.

Decision 03

Home leads with the next night

Free events lose around a third of sign-ups, so Home leads with the next night and Check in instead of a ranking of the most-played games.

Final screens

The screens follow one player back to the same table

12 screens follow one player towards a second night, grouped by the problem they solve. The sign-up flow includes a real night so newcomers see what they are joining.

Look and feel

Nostalgic play replaced ancient Greece

The original Ancient Greek styling needed more detail and accuracy than the timeline and skill set allowed, so the look was switched to ’90s party games, which feel relaxed for a first night. Switching late cost time, and the style hasn’t been tested yet.

Status

Designed, not yet tested

Four interviews and desk research support dropping personality matching. Play-style grouping and “Run it back?” are still assumptions until people book a second night, and no test sessions have run yet.

Looking back

The second night is still untested

Interviewees didn’t want personality matching, and competing apps stop after the first meetup. What’s left to prove is whether people who enjoy a night together keep meeting.
